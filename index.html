Exchanges worth a look

These are placeholders — swap in the exchanges and referral links you actually want to promote. Each card holds a name, a reason, the perk, and a link.

Every platform below falls into one of two fundamentally different categories. Centralized exchanges are companies — Binance, Coinbase, and similar — that hold your funds in their own accounts on your behalf, the way a bank holds a deposit. DeFi exchanges are software: a set of smart contracts that let you trade directly from your own wallet, with no company ever taking custody of anything. That one difference — who's actually holding the asset while you trade — is where most of the practical trade-offs below come from.

Centralized exchanges

Advantages

  • Familiar account-based experience, with support to contact if something goes wrong
  • Built-in bank transfer and card on-ramps for converting real-world currency
  • Typically faster execution and deeper liquidity on major pairs
  • Regulatory oversight in reputable jurisdictions adds a layer of accountability

Disadvantages

  • You don't hold the underlying keys — if the exchange is hacked, freezes withdrawals, or becomes insolvent, your funds are at risk
  • Identity verification trades away a degree of privacy
  • Access can be restricted or frozen based on geography or regulation
  • Coin selection and features are limited to whatever the company chooses to support

DeFi exchanges

Advantages

  • Non-custodial — trades settle directly from a wallet you control
  • Permissionless: generally no sign-up, KYC, or geographic gatekeeping
  • Fully on-chain and auditable — trading and liquidity data is publicly verifiable
  • New or smaller tokens are often available here long before any centralized exchange lists them

Disadvantages

  • No support line — a wrong address or an approved malicious contract can mean an irreversible loss
  • Smart contract risk: even audited protocols have been exploited before
  • No built-in fiat on-ramp — you generally need crypto already, often bought via a centralized exchange first
  • Network ("gas") fees are unpredictable and can spike during congestion

What actually separates a good exchange from a risky one

The same eight questions apply no matter which platform you're weighing up — the ones above or anywhere else. None of this is a ranking of the exchanges on this page; it's the checklist worth running through yourself before you open an account anywhere.

01

Ease of use

A clean, simple buy-and-sell flow matters more than advanced trading tools for most people starting out. Look for a free built-in wallet, clear beginner guides, and features like recurring buys rather than a screen full of options you don't need yet.

02

Security

Two-factor authentication on withdrawals, most customer funds held offline in cold storage, a proper HTTPS connection, and identity verification are the baseline. For any serious holding, moving funds to your own hardware wallet is worth the extra step.

03

Company transparency

A trustworthy exchange is upfront about who runs it, where it's based, and how to reach a real person when something goes wrong. Responsive support is easy to overlook until the moment you actually need it.

04

Fees, including hidden ones

Advertised trading fees don't always tell the whole story — some platforms make up the difference through wider spreads or less favourable conversion rates. It's worth comparing a couple of platforms side by side rather than trusting one "low fees" claim.

05

Range of coins

Every exchange lists Bitcoin and Ethereum. What varies is access to everything past the top few names — worth checking if you expect to want smaller-cap coins later, even if you're only buying majors today.

06

Where it operates

An exchange licensed or based in your own country generally means smoother tax reporting, more relevant support, and clearer recourse if something goes wrong. International exchanges aren't off-limits, just worth using with that trade-off in mind.

07

Spreads & liquidity

The spread — the gap between the buy and sell price — is a real cost that's easy to miss because it isn't itemized like a fee. Higher trading volume generally means tighter spreads and smoother execution, which matters more as trade size grows.

08

Verification & payment methods

Faster identity verification gets you trading sooner, and the deposit options on offer — bank transfer, card, other cryptocurrencies — affect both convenience and whether a hidden currency-conversion fee shows up along the way.

This list draws on the kind of checklist consumer finance sites publish for comparing exchanges — Canstar's guide is a solid one to read in full alongside this.

The themes that tend to come up in every market cycle, framed as background context — not signals to trade on.

Real-world assets

AI & crypto

Networks paying for compute, data, and agents in on-chain tokens rather than traditional invoicing.

Layer 2 scaling

Faster, cheaper networks built on top of established chains, aimed at everyday transaction volume.

Restaking

Putting already-staked assets to work securing additional networks, for an added layer of yield.

Community & meme tokens

Attention-driven assets with little fundamental backing — high volatility either direction, worth naming plainly.

Regulation

Clearer rules on custody, disclosure, and market structure that shape which coins and platforms institutions are willing to touch.

Spot ETFs sit outside the list above on purpose — they're regulated funds that track a coin's price without holding the coin itself, which makes them a different kind of asset from the tokens shown here rather than another narrative within crypto.
